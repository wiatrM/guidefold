# AI writing patterns to remove

Distilled from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" guide (WikiProject AI Cleanup), adapted for general writing: articles, emails, social posts, web copy, reports, scripts. Each entry gives the pattern, how to spot it, and the fix.

A single instance of one pattern is not proof of AI writing, and not every instance needs surgery. Density is the tell. When several patterns cluster in one passage, that passage needs rewriting.

## 1. Content patterns

### 1.1 Significance inflation
AI puffs up importance with stock phrases about legacy, significance and broader trends.

Watch for: stands as / serves as a testament, a vital / crucial / pivotal role or moment, underscores its importance, highlights its significance, reflects broader trends, symbolising its enduring / ongoing / lasting, setting the stage for, marking / shaping, represents a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, indelible mark, deeply rooted, rich heritage.

Fix: keep the fact, delete the fanfare. "The institute was founded in 1989, marking a pivotal moment in regional statistics" → "The institute was founded in 1989."

### 1.2 Superficial analysis clauses
A participle clause bolted to the end of a sentence that pretends to analyse: "..., highlighting the need for change", "..., ensuring long-term success", "..., fostering a sense of community", "..., reflecting its commitment to quality".

Fix: cut the clause. If it contains a genuine fact, make it its own plain sentence.

### 1.3 Promotional puffery
Advertising tone leaking into neutral writing: boasts, vibrant, rich, nestled, in the heart of, breathtaking, renowned, groundbreaking, diverse array, commitment to excellence, seamlessly connects, must-visit, hidden gem.

Fix: neutral wording. "Nestled in the vibrant heart of the city, the hotel boasts 200 rooms" → "The hotel is in the city centre and has 200 rooms."

### 1.4 Vague attributions (weasel wording)
Opinions credited to nobody in particular: experts argue, observers have noted, industry reports suggest, many believe, critics say, it is widely regarded.

Fix: if the original names a source, use it. If not, either state the claim plainly (if it's the author's own view) or cut it. Never invent a source to fill the gap.

### 1.5 Overgeneralised sourcing
Presenting one source as many: "several publications have described..." when only one is cited; "reviewers praised..." citing a single review.

Fix: match the claim to the actual evidence in the original.

### 1.6 Canned "challenges and future outlook" endings
The formula: "Despite its success, X faces several challenges, including... Despite these challenges, X continues to thrive." Often paired with a "Future Prospects" section and a vaguely upbeat final line.

Fix: if the challenges are real content, state them plainly, once. Delete the ritual optimism.

### 1.7 Didactic disclaimers
Lecturing an imagined reader: it's important to note that, it's crucial to remember, it's worth noting, be sure to consult a professional, results may vary.

Fix: delete, or state the point directly without announcing its importance.

### 1.8 Section summaries and formulaic conclusions
Paragraphs that restate what was just said: In summary, In conclusion, Overall, Ultimately, To wrap up. Also headers titled "Conclusion" on short pieces.

Fix: end where the content ends. A closing thought is fine; a recap is not.

## 2. Language patterns

### 2.1 AI vocabulary
Words statistically overused by LLMs. Their presence in numbers is one of the strongest tells:

delve, tapestry (abstract), landscape (abstract), testament, pivotal, crucial, robust, vibrant, meticulous(ly), showcase, underscore (verb), highlight (verb, overused), leverage (verb), foster, garner, intricate / intricacies, interplay, seamless(ly), elevate, enhance, bolster, align with, boasts, enduring, valuable insights, key (adjective, overused), Additionally (sentence-opening), Moreover, Furthermore (overused as sentence-openers), navigate (metaphorical), embark, realm, ever-evolving, fast-paced world, game-changer, unlock, harness, empower, journey (metaphorical).

Fix: swap for the everyday word. delve into → look at; leverage → use; foster → build / encourage; underscores → shows; enhance → improve; robust → strong / reliable; utilise → use; commence → start; prior to → before.

Note: one appearance of one word means nothing. Ten of them in three paragraphs is the signature.

### 2.2 Copula avoidance
AI dodges plain "is/are/has": serves as, stands as, functions as, operates as, represents, features, offers, maintains, refers to (in definitions).

Fix: restore the plain verb. "The gallery serves as the exhibition space and features four rooms" → "The gallery is the exhibition space and has four rooms."

### 2.3 Negative parallelisms
"It's not just X, it's Y." "Not only... but also..." "This isn't about X. It's about Y." "No X, no Y, just Z." "X rather than Y" (repeatedly).

Fix: state the positive claim directly. "It's not just a phone, it's a lifestyle" → whatever the sentence actually means: "The phone has become part of how people organise their lives."

### 2.4 Rule of three
Triplets everywhere: "clear, concise, and compelling"; "strategy, execution, and results"; three-item lists in consecutive sentences.

Fix: vary list lengths. Keep the strongest one or two items. One deliberate triplet is fine; a pattern of them is not.

### 2.5 Elegant variation (synonym cycling)
AI avoids repeating a word, cycling through synonyms: the company / the firm / the organisation / the enterprise, all meaning the same entity in one paragraph.

Fix: humans repeat words. Pick the natural term and reuse it.

### 2.6 Hedge-free superlative avoidance plus stiff euphemisms
AI prefers stiff synonyms: authored → wrote; relocated → moved; utilised → used; attempted → tried; passed away → died (context permitting); individuals → people; numerous → many; approximately → about (in casual registers).

Fix: use the plain word for the register.

## 3. Style and formatting patterns

### 3.1 Em dash overuse
AI leans on em dashes — like this — for emphasis, often several per paragraph, and often where a comma or full stop is natural.

Fix: replace with commas, full stops, colons or parentheses. Keep an em dash only if the author's own style clearly uses them and only sparingly.

### 3.2 Bold overuse
Mechanically bolding **key phrases** throughout, key-takeaways style.

Fix: remove bold from prose. Bold survives only in genuine headings or where the format demands it.

### 3.3 Title Case Headings
"Impact of Technology and Digitalization" style capitalisation of every main word.

Fix: sentence case ("Impact of technology and digitalisation"), unless the destination's house style says otherwise.

### 3.4 Inline-header bullet lists
The signature format:
- **Speed:** The system processes requests quickly.
- **Reliability:** Downtime is rare.

Fix: convert to prose unless a list genuinely aids the reader. If a list stays, drop the bolded label-colon scaffolding.

### 3.5 Emoji as formatting
🚀 Emoji decorating headers or bullets.

Fix: remove unless the author's platform voice genuinely uses them (and then only where they'd plausibly type one).

### 3.6 Unnecessary tables
Small tables presenting two or three facts that read better as a sentence.

Fix: fold into prose.

### 3.7 Curly quotes inconsistency
Mixed “curly” and "straight" quotation marks in one piece can indicate pasted AI output.

Fix: make them consistent with the destination format.

## 4. Leftover chatbot artefacts

Delete on sight; these are copy-paste residue, not writing:

- Conversational framing: "Certainly!", "I hope this helps", "Would you like me to...", "Here's a draft of...", "Let me know if..."
- Knowledge-cutoff disclaimers: "as of my last update", "while specific details are limited", "not widely documented in available sources"
- Placeholder text: "[Your Name]", "[insert date]", "[link to source]", "2025-XX-XX"
- Citation markup residue: "contentReference", "oaicite", "turn0search3", "utm_source=chatgpt.com" in URLs, "[cite: 3]", stray ↩ symbols
- Markdown syntax showing as literal text: **asterisks**, ## hashes, --- breaks in a format that doesn't render them

## 5. What NOT to change

- Facts, figures, names, dates, quotes, citations. These pass through untouched.
- The author's register. Casual stays casual, formal stays formal.
- Correct grammar. Humanising does not mean adding errors, slang or fake typos.
- Words on the watchlists used naturally and sparsely. "The key finding was..." once in a report is normal writing.
- Structure the reader needs. If the piece is a step-by-step guide, numbered steps stay.

## 6. Signs of human writing worth preserving or restoring

Where they fit the meaning, these make text read human:

- Plain is/has constructions: "there is a", "it has a"
- Simple verbs: wrote, moved, used, tried, died
- Direct, definitive statements: "was the first", "is the only" (only if the original supports them)
- Natural hedges: very, perhaps, tends to, quite
- Ordinary connective tissue: "as a result of", "in order to", "the fact that"
- Varied sentence length, including short ones. And occasional fragments, where the register allows.
